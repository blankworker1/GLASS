# A Production Model for Bosa

*What it is, what it is not, and how it should be measured*

---

The first text asked a simple question: what does Bosa make? Many people agreed with the answer. Then came the harder question. How do you recreate the conditions for production in a world where production moves to wherever labour, materials and money are cheapest?

This text is an answer. It is not a product. It is a way of operating.

Bosa will never win a race to the lowest cost. So it should not enter that race. The model below is designed to work without winning it.

## The starting point: make only what is already wanted

Most businesses make first and sell afterwards. That is exactly what requires cheap labour, cheap materials and cheap money, because the producer carries all the risk: paying for stock in advance, storing it, and cutting prices on whatever does not sell.

When demand comes first — an order, a subscription, a prepayment — most of that risk disappears. Less capital is needed. Nothing is wasted. The buyer helps finance production. Bosa's higher costs matter far less when it is not carrying stock nobody has asked for.

This is also how the model grows: person to person, buyer to producer, as fast as real demand allows and no faster.

Tourism changes its role. The visitor meets the oil, the wine or the maker in August, and keeps ordering through the winter. Tourism becomes the shop window and the delivery route, not the whole economy.

## What the model is, and what it is not

| It is | It is not |
| --- | --- |
| A cooperative enterprise that makes a profit | A non-profit, an association or a social enterprise |
| Economically viable from the first product | A project that needs time and subsidy to "find its feet" |
| A producer of goods | A service business |
| Running its own services (orders, logistics, marketing) in-house, to serve production | Selling those services as its product |
| Funded by its members and its customers | Dependent on grants or government contracts to stay open |
| Producing against confirmed demand | Producing first and hoping to sell |
| Growing person to person, at the pace of demand | Chasing growth for its own sake |
| Majority owned by local members | Owned or controlled from outside |
| Working all year | A summer business |
| Built to get stronger from shocks | Built only to survive them |

## Why a cooperative

The preferred structure is a cooperative enterprise — a *società cooperativa* that trades and makes a profit like any other business. It is not a social cooperative, an association or a third-sector body. Those are built around non-profit status and, very often, public funding.

A cooperative suits this model for four reasons:

- **One member, one vote.** Control does not follow money. No investor can take over the enterprise by putting in more capital.
- **Rewards follow participation, not capital.** Members share in the surplus according to what they put into the enterprise — the work they do, the goods they supply — not according to how much money they invested.
- **Reserves that stay.** By law, part of every year's profit goes into reserves that cannot be divided among the members. The enterprise grows stronger over time, and that strength cannot be sold off or stripped out.
- **It stays local.** The members decide who joins. Outside capital can take part, but it cannot take control.

## How to measure it: resilience, not size

This is the most important part of the model.

Most businesses measure size and growth: turnover, sales, growth rate. Those numbers encourage exactly the behaviour this model avoids — borrowing to expand, chasing volume, depending on one big customer.

This model measures resilience instead. Most of its measures are **limits**, not targets: a floor it must stay above, or a ceiling it must stay below. A year in which the enterprise stays within every limit is a good year, even if nothing grew.

### Floors — must not fall below

- **Committed sales:** the share of production sold against orders, subscriptions or prepayment before it is made.
- **Recurring income:** the share of fixed costs covered by regular, repeat income.
- **Reserves:** how many months the enterprise could cover its fixed costs with no sales at all.
- **Off-season income:** the share of income earned outside June to September.
- **Local ownership:** always above half.
- **Shared skills:** every essential skill held by at least two or three people. When knowledge sits with one person or one family, it disappears with them. That is how Bosa lost the tanneries.

### Ceilings — must not rise above

- **Concentration:** the share of income from the single biggest customer, sales channel or product, and the share of inputs from the single biggest supplier.
- **Public money:** the share of operating costs paid by grants or public funds. The target is zero.
- **Imported materials:** the share of raw materials brought in from outside the valley.

### Getting stronger from shocks

- **Recovery time:** after any setback — a bad harvest, a lost customer, a member leaving — record how long it takes to get back within the limits. Over the years, this time should get shorter.
- **Options:** how many different products or uses the same building, equipment and materials can support. The more options, the less any single failure can stop the whole enterprise.
- **A yearly stress test:** once a year, ask what would happen if the biggest channel disappeared, or the summer failed. Write down the answer, then fix whatever would have broken.

### Over five years, at least

- **Year 1:** measure the starting point and set the first limits.
- **Years 2 to 5:** tighten the limits step by step.
- **Each year's report** does not say "we grew". It says "we stayed within every limit, and we recover faster than we did last year."

## Choosing what to make: the decision framework

No product is chosen in advance. Every proposal, from anyone, goes through the same six tests in the same order. A proposal that fails a test is not thrown away: it is set aside until conditions change, or redesigned until it passes. Only a product that passes all six goes forward.

1. **Materials.** The main raw materials come from the valley — grown, raised, or currently thrown away. Anything brought in from outside is a small part of the whole, and available from more than one supplier.
2. **Energy.** Its energy needs are known, and the valley can supply them.
3. **Water and waste.** It takes from the Temo and the land no more than they can replace. What it leaves behind is safe, or becomes the raw material for something else. The tanneries ended partly because of what they left behind.
4. **Place.** It can be made in existing spaces — a conca, a garage, a unit in the zona artigianale, a farm building — that can be permitted for that use.
5. **Skills.** The skills it needs exist in the valley or can be learned here, and can be held by more than one person.
6. **The model.** It is viable from the first batch, made against confirmed demand, sold all year round, and needs no public money to keep going.

The answers to each test are written down for every proposal, so decisions can be compared, revisited and explained.
